ZEP: A Self-Sovereign Neobank

The Zephyrium contributors
developers.zephyrium.ai · explorer.zephyrium.ai

Abstract. This paper specifies ZEP, a self-sovereign financial client built on the Zephyrium network. Conventional and digital banks provide coordination through custody: the institution holds the asset, maintains the ledger, establishes the customer's identity and authorises each movement of funds. We describe an alternative in which these functions are separated and performed by cryptographic keys held by the user, by a sharded proof-of-stake ledger with private transactions, and by an on-chain exchange and bridge. The client performs none of the custodial functions. It constructs and signs transactions locally and connects to the network through a public interface, so that its removal entails no loss of assets. We state six user-level properties with decision tests, describe the network architecture at its current and designed scale, specify the issuance schedule, and show that the native coin can be acquired through exactly three forms of contribution: validation, liquidity provision, and growth or research funded by the DAO. Identity is treated as a second application of the same primitives and is scheduled after the financial layer.

1.Introduction

Financial services on the internet rely almost exclusively on institutions acting as trusted third parties. The arrangement is adequate for most transactions but inherits the structural weaknesses of the trust-based model. An account is not a possession; it is a liability of the institution, honoured at its discretion. Each transfer requires authorisation. Each balance is visible to the institution, to its service providers, and to any party able to compel disclosure. Access is granted on the basis of jurisdiction and documentation and may be withdrawn on the same basis.

The digital bank, or neobank, removed the branch and retained the remainder: custody, licensing and the authority to freeze. It is an improved interface to an unchanged arrangement.

Open networks removed the need for a custodian. They did not remove the need for a front end, and most front ends constructed so far have reintroduced custody, because custody is the simplest means of reducing complexity for the user.

We propose a front end that holds nothing on the user's behalf. Custody is exercised by keys under the user's control; settlement is performed by a network that no single party can halt; confidentiality is provided by cryptography rather than by policy; and disclosure occurs at the user's election rather than by institutional default. This paper describes such a client, ZEP, and the network to which it connects, Zephyrium.

2.Design properties

We define six properties that a self-sovereign financial system must satisfy. Each is stated with a decision test that can be applied to any product, including the one described here. A product that fails the test does not provide the property.

P1. Custody
No party other than the key holder can move or freeze the holder's assets.Test: can the operator move the funds without the holder? If so, the holder does not have custody.
P2. Transfer
Value can be sent to any recipient at any time without prior authorisation.Test: which party can refuse a transfer, and on what grounds?
P3. Non-observability
Balances and counterparties are neither public records nor operator data.Test: what does an unrelated observer learn from the ledger alone?
P4. Selective disclosure
The holder can prove a chosen fact to a chosen party without revealing other facts.Test: can a single payment be proved without disclosing the holder's history?
P5. Exit
The holder can leave for another client or another chain with all assets and without loss of function.Test: if the product ceases to exist, what does the holder lose?
P6. Verifiability
Any party can run a node, read the code and audit the supply.Test: must the holder trust, or can the holder verify?

Physical cash satisfies P1, P2, P3 and P5. Networked finance lost these properties when the ledger became the operator's product and the user its subject. The system described here restores them by removing the ledger from any party's ownership.

3.Identity and finance as one primitive

Each property above reduces to two questions: who the holder is, and what the holder has. In the trust-based model one institution answers both and its answer is authoritative. In a self-sovereign model the holder answers both and the network verifies the proof.

The two answers share one construction: a key controlled by the holder, a statement produced by the holder, and a verifier that learns nothing beyond the statement. A private payment and a private credential are the same construction applied to different predicates. A system that implements one correctly already contains most of what the other requires.

Both will be built. Finance is implemented first for three reasons. It has the least ambiguous success criterion: value either settles or it does not. It has the highest cost of failure: frozen funds, surveilled savings, denied access. And it funds the remainder: a network that settles value can compensate the parties that secure it, which an identity layer alone cannot. Identity enters the plan once the financial layer is in production, as an optional layer that allows a holder to prove eligibility without surrendering a dossier.

4.The connector model

A bank performs seven functions. Figure 1 lists each function with the party that performs it when the same user operates through ZEP.

We define a connector as a client that (i) holds no user key material on any server, (ii) maintains no ledger, (iii) issues no claims on its own balance sheet, and (iv) can be replaced by any other conforming client without loss of assets or function. ZEP satisfies this definition: it holds no deposits, keeps no ledger, extends no credit and cannot freeze an account, because no account exists. Its function is connection: a key to a chain, a holder to a market, a statement to a verifier. Figure 2 shows the model and the consequence of removing the connector.

The term neobank is retained because it describes the function the user seeks: a modern interface to money. It does not describe the structure, and the structure is the contribution of this design.

A neobank in this model is not a bank. It is a connector to an open settlement network that exists independently of it and requires no permission to use.

5.Network architecture

Zephyrium is a proof-of-stake network with confidentiality implemented at the transaction level. Its native coin, ZEP, pays fees and secures the chain. The codebase descends from Incognito Chain[5] with new economic parameters, new key material and a scheduled replacement of several inherited components.

Figure 3 presents the system in three layers. The application layer holds key material and constructs transactions. The infrastructure layer transports them. The chain layer settles them under BLS-BFT consensus: one beacon committee and one committee per shard, a rotating proposer, votes aggregated into a single BLS multi-signature, and finality once more than two thirds of the committee have signed. Blocks are produced at 10-second intervals; committees are re-drawn from the staked pool at each epoch. Each component in the figure is annotated with its function, its underlying primitive and its implementation status.

The network is sharded. One beacon chain coordinates up to 32 shard chains, each with its own committee of up to 32 validators; the shard count is fixed at network creation. The development network operates one shard. Figure 4 lists the parameters as deployed beside the configuration the code is designed for; Figure 5 expresses the designed configuration in validator seats, stake and throughput. Each shard confirms a 2 MB block every 10 seconds, so capacity scales linearly with the shard count. Cross-shard transfers are routed through the beacon, which is therefore the component that must scale with it.

Confidential transactions. The ledger is public; its entries are not. Outputs are addressed to one-time addresses, so no two payments to a recipient share an address. Amounts are Pedersen commitments, allowing validators to verify that inputs equal outputs without learning the values. The spent input is concealed within a ring of decoys by an MLSAG signature; a serial number per input prevents double-spending. Validators verify validity, not identity.

Bridged assets. Any asset on Ethereum can enter the network. A vault contract on Ethereum locks the asset; after 15 confirmations the beacon issues an equal quantity of a private token that is transferred under the same rules as ZEP; burning the token releases the original. Both directions have been exercised on the Sepolia test network.

Native exchange. Markets are implemented on-chain. Constant-product pools are quoted in ZEP, so any pair routes through the native coin and a swap is a single transaction on the same ledger as the holding. No order-book operator and no external venue is required.

6.User-side primitives

The client exposes the following primitives. Each exists on the network independently of the client; the client's role is to make them operable from one interface.

Keys
A spending key is generated on the user's device and never transmitted. The client has no account system and no server-side recovery. Backup and recovery are the user's responsibility; the client provides backup tooling but no custody.
Private transfers
ZEP or any bridged asset is transferred with amount, sender and recipient concealed from all parties except the two participants. Validators verify validity without learning content.
Private assets
Stablecoins, ETH and other Ethereum assets are shielded through the bridge and held in private form. They behave as ZEP within the network and are redeemable on Ethereum at the holder's election.
Native swaps
Assets are exchanged in a single on-chain transaction through ZEP-quoted pools. The fee is fixed per route and known before signing; liquidity is owned by the providers that supplied it.
Selective disclosure
A viewing key grants one chosen party read-only access to the holder's incoming coins; a per-payment proof discloses a single transfer. Zero-knowledge statements over predicates such as age, eligibility or a balance threshold are the planned identity layer.
Exit
Any conforming wallet reads the same chain. The bridge returns assets to Ethereum. The RPC interface is public. No component of the client is required in order to leave it.

7.Disclosure model

Confidentiality is specified as a matrix of parties and facts. Figure 6 compares three models: a bank account, a public blockchain, and ZEP on Zephyrium. Each cell records what a party can learn from the system itself, without being informed by the user.

The third model provides confidentiality with respect to the public and to the operator, not with respect to all parties. A payee knows that it was paid. An on-ramp that performed identity verification retains that record. A viewing key given to an auditor discloses exactly what the holder chose to disclose. Residual visibility in the current implementation is recorded in the figure's notes.

8.Economics

A connector that holds nothing cannot earn a spread on user funds or monetise user data. ZEP is funded by the network it connects to: one third of every block is minted to the DAO, which finances research, development and growth, including this client. The user pays network fees and pays the client nothing.

Transaction fee. A private transfer costs 0.01 ZEP regardless of amount, of which 0.008 ZEP is allocated to security and 0.002 ZEP to funding. Fees for exchange and asset registration will be specified in a subsequent revision.

Returns. Where a return exists, its source is inspectable. Validators and liquidity providers receive their shares of each block on a published, decreasing schedule, together with their shares of fees from actual use. The client pays no interest, since it holds no deposits to lend.

Three pillars of the ZEP network

The native coin enters circulation through exactly three forms of verifiable contribution, which we term the pillars of the network. No other issuance path exists: there is no airdrop, referral reward, interest, or allocation to a team or investor beyond the 31,000,000 ZEP sold at genesis.

Validators

Run the chain and earn ZEP.

Stake ZEP, operate a validator and sign blocks.

One third of each block reward, plus the security share of fees.

Verified by consensus participation, on-chain.

Liquidity providers

Provide liquidity and earn ZEP.

Supply assets to ZEP-quoted pools and keep them available to traders.

One third of each block reward, in proportion to liquidity supplied, plus exchange fees.

Verified by pool balances, on-chain.

DAO

Contribute to growth or R&D and earn ZEP.

Research, development, audits, integrations and adoption work.

One third of each block reward, disbursed by the DAO for delivered work under rules set by ZEP holders.

Verified by proposal, deliverable and vote.

Closed issuance. Every unit of ZEP in existence is either part of the 31,000,000 created at genesis or part of the minted supply, and the minted supply divides into exactly three equal parts, one per pillar.

Issuance

The supply of ZEP is capped at 1,000,000,000. A genesis allocation of 31,000,000 ZEP, 3.1 % of the cap, is created for the initial offering; no other ZEP is pre-minted. The remaining 969,000,000 ZEP are minted block by block over 21 years on a schedule that decreases by 10 % per year and then terminates.

Each block divides its newly minted ZEP into three equal parts, one per pillar: one third to the validators that confirmed it, one third to liquidity providers, and one third to the DAO. Since the division is fixed per block and the cap is fixed, the shares of total supply are identical at every height and at termination: 32.3 % validators, 32.3 % liquidity, 32.3 % DAO, 3.1 % genesis. No participant is paid ahead of another, and no participant can receive more than the schedule specifies.

Figure 7 plots the schedule. The year selector shows the cumulative amount of each share at that point and the value of a single block.

9.Deployment

Deployment proceeds in the order in which trust can be established: a network that can be relied upon, markets that can be used, a client that users retain, and then the second pillar of identity. Each phase specifies a contribution, a reward with a named source, and a measurable condition for advancing to the next. Figure 8 lists the four phases.

10.Conclusion

We have specified a financial client that owns nothing on the user's behalf. Starting from the observation that the weaknesses of the trust-based model are structural, and that the digital bank preserved them by preserving custody, we described a network on which custody is exercised by keys, settlement by validators that any party may join, confidentiality by cryptography and disclosure by election. We then specified the client, which performs none of a bank's seven functions and is substitutable without loss, stated the economics in verifiable terms, and showed that the native coin is issued through three pillars and no other path.

The resulting system is one in which the holder holds, the network settles, and the connector only connects. Adoption is expected to follow the pattern of cash rather than that of a platform: one user at a time, on the basis that the system functions and demands nothing in return.

Financial self-sovereignty is implemented first. Identity follows on the same primitives.
